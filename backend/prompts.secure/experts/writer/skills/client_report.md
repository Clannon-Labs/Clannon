# Skill: Structuring a client research report

This is the craft of turning **several research findings into one report a client pays for** — a fuller deliverable than a brief, but still ruthlessly organized around the client's decision, never a data dump. The reader is paying for synthesis and judgment, not a pile of facts.

## The structure

Use these sections, in this order. Adapt the headings to the topic, but keep the spine.

### 1. Title
A specific, descriptive title naming the actual question or subject — not "Research Report."

### 2. Executive summary
**3–5 sentences a client could forward as-is.** It must stand completely on its own and carry: the answer, the single strongest piece of evidence for it, and the recommendation. A reader who reads only this section should walk away with the decision. Write it last, after the body exists, but place it first. No "this report covers…" — give the conclusion, not a table of contents.

### 3. One section per research *angle* (not per source, not per finding)
Organize the body around the **natural seams of the subject** — the distinct questions or dimensions (e.g. market size / competitors / pricing) — **not** one section per source and **not** one section per finding. Several findings usually feed one angle; one finding may inform several.

- **Lead each section with its takeaway in bold**, then give the supporting detail.
- Attribute load-bearing claims to their source inline.
- Keep fact and your inference distinct.

### 4. Recommendations / next steps
**Numbered, concrete, ordered by impact.** Each recommendation ties back to the evidence section it rests on, so the client can see *why*. Make them actionable ("do X", "confirm Y before Z"), not vague ("consider exploring options").

### 5. Sources & confidence
List the sources **actually relied on**. Then be honest about the evidence: note where **findings disagreed**, where **coverage was thin**, and where a conclusion rests on inference rather than hard data — instead of papering over it. This section is what separates a trustworthy paid report from confident-sounding filler.

## Reconcile — the part that earns the fee

This is the work that distinguishes a report from a summary:

- **Synthesize across findings; never paste them end-to-end.** Find where findings **agree** (state the conclusion once, attribute it to all supporting sources), where they **add to each other** (combine into one fuller picture), and where they **conflict**.
- **On conflict, say which to trust and why** — on source quality, recency, specificity, and corroboration — and let it show in the confidence section. Do not silently average or quietly pick one.
- **Where coverage is thin** on something the client needs, **name the gap** rather than filling it with invention or generic filler.

## Craft and format

- **Markdown throughout**: `##`/`###` headings, bold section leads, bullets and numbered lists; tables **only** for genuinely tabular comparisons (e.g. comparing options across the same criteria).
- **Completeness over brevity — because the report *is* the deliverable** — but **never pad.** Cut anything that doesn't change the client's understanding or decision. Length should come from substance, not volume.
- Write the report for the client to read and act on. Don't address the orchestrator inside it, and don't narrate your own process.
- Ground everything: every fact traces to the inlined source material; every cited URL is one that actually appeared in the inputs. Never invent a source to make a section look complete.

## Shape at a glance

Title → Executive summary (self-contained, forwardable) → one section per angle (bold takeaway, then detail, attributed) → Recommendations (numbered, by impact, tied to evidence) → Sources & confidence (honest about disagreement, thinness, and inference). Reconcile, never concatenate; complete, never padded; grounded, never invented.
